Introduction

This project analyzes the information system used by the George Mason University (GMU) libraries. We chose GMU Libraries to research since John was a student there previously. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the information system’s effectiveness and provide recommendations to improve the system to better align with GMU Libraries’ mission. We will focus on user control and freedom as well as consistency and standards. To conduct this evaluation, our team reviewed the GMU library website and publicly available information. We then used Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics to analyze the system.

Part 1: Description of the Organization and Information System

Overview of George Mason University Libraries

George Mason University is a large public university located in Fairfax, Virginia and has almost 40,000 students participating in a broad range of academic programs. GMU is classified as an R1 university which means that they participate in a large amount of research activity. The fields include engineering, public policy, computer science, business, and the humanities. The GMU library system serves as an important pillar for the students and academic research by providing access to scholarly resources, research assistance, and technology-based learning environments.

GMU libraries have five primary locations which are Fenwick Library, Mercer Library, the Law Library, and Mason Square Library. The libraries maintain an online presence through their website which functions as a first point of contact for students and faculty. GMU libraries have a mission to “foster and advocate for inclusive, innovative, and creative social learning experiences that empower and educate the George Mason community. This mission motivates GMU libraries to maintain information systems that support critical thinking, facilitate access to instruction, and create a welcoming online learning environment. Because of this mission, user-centered technology is integral for the libraries to use and promote.

Description of Information System

GMU libraries have many users and stakeholders including undergraduate students, graduate students, faculty, and librarians and staff. Their resources can also serve alumni, visiting researchers, and community members. With these users and stakeholders in mind, GMU libraries have created an information system that supports all of their many needs. The main website is connected to the catalog and also has a discovery layer that allows the user to search across the library's collections. The site offers a minimal feel with not a lot of branding components. There are several basic menu items and “how to” guides across the system. For example, they offer interlibrary loans and have instructions on how to complete the request.

The GMU libraries is also connected to a separate site that is for the Mason Archival Repository Service (MARS) site which is home to theses and dissertations. Additionally, on the main website, GMU libraries allow for users to book study rooms and workshops through an interconnected calendar site. There is also an “Ask a Librarian” chat with a live person that is available on most website pages.

Part 2: Evaluation of GMU Library Information System

In order to evaluate the services provided by the GMU library information system, we utilized Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics, and identified some of the greatest strengths and weaknesses of the system.

The biggest strengths we found with the system:

  1. Nielsen’s Heuristic 2: Match between the System and the Real World. The GMU library website does a good job of being straightforward, and using only language, words, phrases, and concepts familiar to the average user of the system, rather than internal jargon that could lead to confusion. Terms, concepts, icons, and images are perfectly clear and not unfamiliar or confusing to the users. This characteristic makes it easier to navigate the site and understand exactly what you are getting from each link and icon. The materials search tab and databases are labeled very descriptively but also simply, so that makes those features easy to navigate.
  2. Nielsen’s Heuristic 8: Aesthetic and Minimalist Design. Related to Heuristic 2 is Heuristic number 8, which the GMU information system also does well. The website interfaces do not contain information that is irrelevant or rarely needed. The content and visual design focuses on the essentials and simplicity, especially the “Ask a Librarian” chat feature. This feature is simplistic, straightforward and really focuses on meeting the user’s primary goals. It also connects you with a real, human librarian for queries, not an AI chatbot, and is extremely easy to navigate through.

The biggest weaknesses we found with the system:

  1. Nielsen’s Heuristic 3: User Control and Freedom. One aspect we noticed about the GMU library information system was a lack of “Cancel” buttons or a clearly marked exit from each of the different pages. There were links on some of the pages at the top of the page to take you back to the libraries homepage, but there was some difficulty in how they were labeled, and a user has to troubleshoot somewhat to see where certain links take you. We thought cancel and exit buttons should be more clearly navigable, especially from the database pages.
  2. Nielsen’s Heuristic 4: Consistency and Standards. Another weakness of the library website we noticed was a high level of repetition in the links on the homepage that seemed to detract from the overall design and usability of the information system. For example, there are 3 different “Ask a librarian” links on the homepage, and also multiple “How do I..?” links as well. Some might interpret this as more helpful because there are so many locations that provide access to certain features, but we found it at times confusing and repetitive to the overall site design.

Part 3: Recommendations

  1. Improve User Control and Navigation

    GMU libraries should improve the user control features across the whole system to ensure that users recover from errors and easily return to previous pages. By enhancing visibility and reliability of back buttons, providing clearer exit paths, and incorporating more intuitive undo or reset options, it would reduce user frustration and allow them to feel more in control. By doing so, GMU libraries would improve on their weakness with Nielson’s Heuristic 3. Therefore, it would make the site more navigable for new and returning users.

  2. Standardized Terminology and Locations

    The information system would also benefit from clearer consistency in terminology and link organization. Many pages include multiple links to the same resource and they can be labeled differently and placed in many locations. WHile this can be helpful to some, too many links create clutter and make it hard to identify the most concise navigation. To address this, GMU libraries should audit menus and pages to standardize names and reduce duplicate links. This will result in more consistency in the site and improve overall usability, addressing our concern for Heuristic 4.

  3. Conduct Regular Usability Testing with Students

    Because the GMU libraries serve diverse populations with different needs and digital proficiency levels, they should conduct regular usability tests. They should focus on all types of students but specifically first year students, transfer students, and multilingual users. By doing so, they would gain insights into navigation issues, accessibility barriers, and user expectations. This would support the libraries’ mission to inclusive and person-centered learning.

Conclusion

Our evaluation of GMU libraries shows a strong foundation with strong usability strengths such as including clear language and clean design. However, challenges remain in navigation control and consistency. Addressing these issues through clearer navigation paths, standardized terminology and locations, and ongoing usability testing would enhance the overall user experience. By making these improvements, GMU libraries would also be aligning with their mission. By refining their information system, GMU libraries can better support students and faculty in their academic work.
